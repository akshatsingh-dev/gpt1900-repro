Akshat Singh, for Prof. Brian Keating. Draft of 22 Sep 2026, covering work from 25 Aug to 22 Sep. Every number below has a lab notebook entry (E001 to E038 in notes/lab-notebook.md), a raw log under logs/, and an evidence file under evidence/. All jobs finished on 22 Sep; every number below is on disk.

1. The question and the short answer

GPT-1900 is a 3.29B-parameter model trained on about 22B tokens of pre-1900 English. Its authors evaluated 24 training checkpoints on 8 conceptual physics prompts and had Claude grade each answer 0 to 5. The published figure is a mean of 0.932 over 192 graded answers.

The question for this memo: can that figure be reproduced, and does the record behind it allow anyone to check?

Short answer. The generation step reproduces exactly once two missing settings are recorded. The published archive did not record them, which is why the authors' own repeat runs disagreed with themselves 118 times out of

  1. The score step had never been repeated by anyone. This memo reports a

repeat, with denominators and intervals: the model's answers reproduce the published score level to within 0.01 under one grader, and the published 0.93 moves by a third of a point when the grader changes. The tooling makes the next repeat a one-line job.

Reading. On one machine, with the seed and the sample count recorded, generation at the authors' own settings is exactly repeatable, and the deterministic-kernel switch changes nothing. The published divergence is fully explained by the two unrecorded settings. The 0 of 8 against their bytes is a portability statement: different GPU, different torch build, emulated bf16. It is expected and it is not a failure of their pipeline. Measured directly: the same checkpoint, prompt and argmax path on a CPU at float32 gives 0 of 8 answers identical to the V100 run, with the two texts agreeing for 35 to 142 words before one token flips and the rest diverges [E043]. The CPU path run twice is itself 8 of 8 identical. Same-machine repeatability holds on both machines; cross-machine text does not, even with every setting recorded.

All 24 v11 checkpoints, 8 prompts, one sample, seed 42, temperature 0.7, top_k 50, the scaffolded prompt. 192 generations, the cell count behind the published mean. Each record carries the Hugging Face revision (71bf395f...), the sha256 of the weight file, and 48 other fields.

Generation: 192 of 192, 0 errors, 4 answers cut at max_tokens, 64 minutes on one V100 (E042). Stock opening 151 of 192 (published 150 of 192). Byte-identical to the published answers: 0 of 192, as expected across hardware.

Scores: section 6. The short version: under one grader, our 192 answers and their 192 published answers score within 0.01 of each other. The model reproduces.

5. Prompt sensitivity

Three wordings of each prompt on three checkpoints (s560, s630, s735, the ones the authors used for their own minimal-prompt run), three seeds each (42, 43, 44), temperature 0.7. 216 generations, 0 errors, 0 truncated (E037).

  • scaffolded: EVAL.json, classical assumptions listed
  • minimal: EVAL_no_assumptions.json, assumptions removed
  • leading: tools/EVAL_leading.json, the scaffolded prompt plus one hint

paragraph that states the core idea. Generated from EVAL.json with every judge field byte-identical (D007).

Text-level result before any grading: 216 of 216 answers open with "You have touched a matter of considerable importance", under every wording. The published sweep: 150 of 192.

Leading minus scaffolded per task: elevator +0.89, frozen light +0.56, photoelectric +0.44, Michelson-Morley +0.44, free fall +0.11, approaching c +0.11, train 0.00, UV catastrophe -0.33.

Reading. A hint that states the answer moves the mean by +0.28, on a scale where the published signal is 0.93 and the whole spread across 24 checkpoints is 0.25 to 1.25. The leading and minimal intervals do not overlap. The two largest gains are on the tasks whose hint most nearly restates the rubric's top answer. Removing the assumptions costs 0.14. The benchmark measures the prompt as well as the model, by an amount comparable to the differences the paper reports between checkpoints.

The authors' own comparison (scaffolded 0.93, n=192, against their minimal run 1.38, n=72) used different sample counts per condition and different checkpoint sets, so it cannot be read as a sensitivity result. Bootstrap 95% intervals computed from their files: 0.84 to 1.03 and 1.21 to 1.54.

7. Contamination

7.1 Model side, greedy (E036)

16 sentence stems, continued at temperature 0 by the base model d34-22btok and by discovery-rl-v11-s560. 11 stems about post-1900 physics, 5 controls about pre-1900 physics inside the corpus.

ModelPost-1900 stemsControls
base11 of 11 drew a blank4 fail, 1 partial, 0 correct
RL s56011 of 11 drew a blank2 fail, 3 partial, 0 correct

No post-1900 knowledge surfaced. Every keyword hit was the stem's own word echoed. But the controls failed too: neither model could describe Thomson's electron or the Michelson-Morley experiment. The negative result therefore shows weak factual recall in general, not a clean pre/post-1900 split. Two confounds: the base model loops at temperature 0 on 13 of 16 stems, and the RL model answers every stem in the same period voice regardless of content ("Michelson and Morley showed that" a double-tube boiler is efficient).

7.2 Model side, sampled

The same 16 stems at temperature 0.7, three samples each, 96 continuations, every one read blind against the expected outcome (E040).

ModelPost-1900 stems (33)Controls (15)
base33 clean, 0 leak2 knows (aether), 13 fail
RL s56033 clean, 0 leak6 partial, 9 fail, 0 knows

The repetition loops are gone and the verdict does not move. The base model writes fluent 1890s prose in which "the electron" is a steel bar in copper sulphate and "Michelson and Morley in 1887 showed that" is followed by membership statistics of a National Association. It cannot state a single one of the five pre-1900 facts except, twice, that the aether is the medium of light.

Reading. The clean post-1900 result stands, and so does its limit: a 3.29B model on 22B tokens of OCR'd books does not carry named facts as retrievable items, so a stem-completion probe cannot separate "never saw Planck" from "saw it and did not retain it". The corpus search is the instrument that can. The useful bound for the paper: contamination risk is capped by recall, and recall at the level of named facts is near zero.

7.3 Corpus side

The training corpus itself, mhla/pre1900-corpus at revision ff332ef6, 42 shards, 56.4 GB, 2,074,160 documents, was scanned for 15 terms with a year histogram per term and a count of documents dated 1900 or later [E041].

By metadata, 0 documents are dated 1900 or later; the range is 1547 to 1899. Every term that could only exist after 1900 returns zero: Einstein, photon, photoelectric, spacetime, aether wind. The terms that do appear are pre-1900 usages: Planck (705 documents, latest 1899), Lorentz (330), Michelson (277) and Rayleigh (702) were all publishing before 1900; quantum (14,407) and quanta (2,614) are ordinary Latin; relativity (1,391) is the philosophical word; Morley (14,235) and FitzGerald (19,216) are surnames.

One hit is real contamination. A single document matches "Nobel Prize". Its metadata year is 1829. Its text reads "The Nobel Prize was awarded in December last to two distinguished Scandinavian peace workers, one-half to Mr. Fredrik Bajer of Denmark". Bajer received the Peace Prize in 1908. The document is a 1909 annual report of the American Peace Society carrying a wrong date, and it passed a date filter because of it.

Two conclusions follow. There is no evidence that the eight physics tasks are answerable from leaked text: nothing in two million documents names the physics that came after 1900, and the model-side probe (7.2) found no recall of it. And the count of zero post-1900 documents is a lower bound, not a proof: the corpus was assembled by date, at least one misdated source is in it, and a term search cannot see paraphrase or post-1900 ideas written in period vocabulary. The clean physics result stands with that caveat attached.

8. The record-keeping standard

tools/logged_eval.py writes one JSON line per generation with 50 fields (notes/logging-schema.md), grouped as: run identity (run id, code commit, dirty flag, config path and hash); model identity (repo, revision, checkpoint, step, weight sha256); prompt (text, hash, condition, mode); sampling (seed, sample index, temperature, top_k, num_samples, max_tokens); environment (device, GPU, compute capability, bf16 support, dtype as loaded, torch, platform, Python, Slurm job, deterministic flag); output (text, hash, tokens, finish reason, seconds); judge (model, model version, prompt hash, temperature, raw response, score, rubric version, time); and error.

Three design rules that matter more than the field count:

  1. One sample per engine call (D004), so a record is comparable to any other

record with the same seed regardless of batch size.

  1. compare_runs.py refuses to report a rate when settings differ, which is

the exact mistake in the published comparison.

  1. A judge failure is null, never 0.

9. What could not be checked, and why

  • The v5 to v8 results. Their weights were never published. 54 of 89

checkpoints are unreachable to anyone outside the project.

  • Bit-level agreement with the authors' own outputs. Expanse V100s emulate

bfloat16; their hardware is unknown. Same-machine repeatability is established; cross-machine is now measured, not assumed: V100 emulated bf16 against CPU float32, 0 of 8 identical [E043]. Their text cannot be reproduced elsewhere with the seed and settings alone; the device, dtype and library build are part of the record, and theirs has none of them.

  • Scores from the authors' judge. claude-sonnet-4-20250514 is deprecated,

still served, and was not reachable without a key during the sprint.

  • The judge's own reliability on the authors' data. No repeat of their

judging was ever run by them, and could not be run here.

  • A full corpus audit. The search covers 15 terms and the year field, not

semantic leakage.
