The stone in falling upon it is not merely released; it is lifted up, and its gravitational impulse upon the stone carries it forward. It is not merely the stone that falls, but the stone and the weight together.

This is not trivial. In a house falling from a sloping roof, we observe something more significant: the whole structure itself vibrates and trembles. The roof, in its moment of rest, has given way under its own compression. The rafters, the beams, the very walls are thrown out of balance. The whole structure trembles -- and with it, the very air within our dwelling, the lungs themselves, and the whole machinery of life. This vibration and strain may be felt by the occupants, but the effect is felt more deeply still. It is not a simple failing of gravity.

Now, I would push the analogy further still. A man who falls freely from a roof does not simply tumble -- he is carried along with his own weight. He falls, in a sense, from his own body. But this carries with it something altogether different. A man sliding down an incline, say from a height to a narrow platform, bears the weight of his whole frame in addition to his own. He moves through space under a pressure -- and the pressure is transmitted through his own constitution.

The weight of the body, in that case, is not a mere inconvenience to be endured; it is the whole arrangement of muscle and blood and nerve. When a man falls, that arrangement is pressed upon. His whole constitution is in motion. This is not a mere motion -- it is a force, acting upon him. And the nature of that force depends upon the quality of the body itself. It is not a uniform pressure even in health. It varies with the bulk and the constitution of the individual. Both influences play upon the eventual motion of the falling body.

This is not a trivial distinction. It teaches us something of the nature of life and of the body's own behaviour. The weight of the body does not simply announce itself when a man falls. It signals itself by its effect. And that effect is always felt more intensely than the mere mechanical falling of a stone.
